# Fragmented Competitive Markets: Historical Marketing Analogues for Chinese AI Labs

**Date:** 2026-05-26
**Author:** MiniMax Marketing Research
**Classification:** Open research

---

## Executive Summary

Chinese AI labs — DeepSeek, Qwen (Alibaba), GLM (Zhipu/z.ai), MiniMax, and emerging entrants — face a classic marketing problem that predates the AI industry by decades. The labs are engaged in a recursive benchmark war: every 3-6 months one lab releases a model that claims top position, only to be displaced by another's release. This dynamic is not unique to AI. Four or more competitors, no established leader with durable market position, noise on social platforms, and each player bleeding into the next — this pattern has appeared across industries, and the outcomes offer specific, actionable marketing lessons.

This report examines four historical analogues where multiple competitors (more than four) competed without a dominant leader, with a specific focus on **marketing differentiation strategies** — not product strategy, distribution, or pricing. The goal is to identify how labs that did not have distribution advantages won through marketing.

The core finding: in markets where no player commands 60%+ share and the product category is young and rapidly evolving, **narrative ownership and identity positioning are more decisive than benchmark leadership**. The lab that owns a specific mental space in the developer community's mind — not "best benchmark" but "the tool for X kind of developer doing Y" — will win, regardless of SWE-bench rankings.

## Introduction

### The Problem in Context

The Chinese AI lab landscape in May 2026 exhibits the following marketing-relevant characteristics:

- **5+ active competitors** with comparable model capabilities: DeepSeek (V4), Qwen 3.6, GLM 5.1, MiniMax M2.7, and upcoming releases from Baidu and ByteDance
- **3-6 month release cycles** — each launch generates a spike of social media discussion, benchmark comparisons, and technical coverage, followed by rapid noise decay
- **No established narrative ownership** — no single lab has established "the go-to model for [specific use case]" in developer communities outside China
- **MIT-licensed models** — all major labs have adopted permissive licensing, removing the proprietary lock-in that might otherwise support a premium narrative
- **Benchmark-driven coverage** — tech media and analyst coverage focuses almost exclusively on SWE-bench, agentic benchmarks, and intelligence indices, creating a narrative cycle that rewards benchmarking over developer outcomes

This dynamic benefits no one. The labs collectively spend more on model development to achieve marginal benchmark improvements that are erased within months. The developer community absorbs noise without clear purchasing signals. And no single lab builds the kind of narrative ownership that translates into durable preference.

### What This Report Is Not

This report does not address product strategy, API distribution, enterprise contract negotiation, or pricing models. The labs do not control the distribution levers in this scenario — the question is specifically about marketing narrative differentiation in a market where multiple competitors are roughly equivalent on technical benchmarks.

### Research Scope

This report examines four historical analogues across industries:

1. **Browser Wars II (2004–2012)** — Firefox vs Chrome vs IE; focus on how Chrome's performance narrative displaced Mozilla's open-source credibility story
2. **VHS vs Betamax (1975–1988)** — Sony vs JVC; focus on how VHS built a narrative around consumer freedom vs Sony's technical superiority framing
3. **Craft Beer Movement (2010–2020)** — thousands of competing breweries; focus on how regional and narrative identity brands grew faster than price-competitive macro-brewery alternatives
4. **Korean Hallyu Wave (2003–2020)** — KBS, MBC, SBS, and entertainment labels; focus on how different broadcast labels built distinct narrative identities in a three-network competitive landscape

## Analogue 1: Browser Wars II — Performance as a Marketing Claim

### The Arc

The second browser war (2004–2017) began with Mozilla Firefox challenging Internet Explorer, then ended with Google Chrome displacing both. Chrome launched in September 2008 and by 2012 held majority market share. The trajectory is remarkable: Chrome went from ~5% share in 2009 to ~35% by 2012, reaching ~65% by 2017. Firefox dropped from ~30% peak to ~15% by 2017. IE collapsed from ~50% to under 10%.

The conventional explanation is that Chrome won on technical merit — the V8 JavaScript engine was faster. But this explanation is insufficient. Firefox was also demonstrably faster than IE in many benchmarks, yet Firefox never displaced IE. The real marketing lesson is in how Chrome constructed and propagated its performance narrative.

### What Actually Happened

**Mozilla's narrative strategy:** Firefox led with the open-source story — transparency, community-driven development, freedom from Microsoft's control. The narrative was identity-driven: "Firefox is the browser for people who care about the open web." This resonated with power users and developers but created a boundary. Choosing Firefox was a statement about values, not purely about performance.

**Google Chrome's narrative strategy:** Chrome's launch narrative was singularly focused on speed — not "open web" or "freedom from Microsoft." Google's marketing centered on the JavaScript engine benchmark: pages load faster, apps run smoother, the web is better. The claim was concrete, measurable, and most importantly, it was a claim any user could verify through their own experience.

The performance claim was also the anti-Microsoft claim. Chrome was built by Google, a company with credibility on speed and scale (search, infrastructure). The narrative leveraged Google's existing reputation without needing to build a new one. Firefox's open-source credibility, by contrast, required explaining what open-source meant to average users.

### The Marketing Lesson

Mozilla built an identity. Google built a claim. In a market where most users could not distinguish Firefox from IE on a technical level, the performance claim was more broadly accessible than the open-source narrative. The identity story required prior knowledge of why open-source mattered; the performance claim was verifiable from day one.

For Chinese AI labs: the lesson is not "build a faster model." The lesson is "construct a verifiable claim that maps to developer pain." Developers who spend hours debugging slow inference do not need to be told that your model scores 5 points higher on agentic benchmarks. They need to know that inference is 40% faster on their specific use case. Claims that map to verifiable developer experience beat claims that map to benchmark tables.

## Analogue 2: VHS vs Betamax — Technical Superiority as a Losing Narrative

### The Arc

Sony introduced Betamax in 1975 with superior technical specifications: better color reproduction, faster forward-winding, more compact cassette. JVC launched VHS a year later in 1976. Sony was widely expected to win — Betamax was objectively better on every technical metric.

VHS won. By the early 1980s, Betamax had effectively lost the format war despite being the superior product. Sony continued producing Betamax devices until 2016, long after VHS dominated the market.

### What Actually Happened

**Sony's marketing narrative:** Sony marketed Betamax as the premium, professional-grade format — superior technology for discerning consumers. The message was essentially: "Betamax is better, and you should pay more for it because quality matters." Sony sought to own the narrative of technical excellence.

**JVC/VHS marketing narrative:** JVC's strategy was the opposite. VHS was positioned as the format for everyone — affordable, accessible, with longer recording time (2 hours vs Betamax's 1 hour). Rather than selling technical superiority, JVC sold freedom: freedom to record movies, sports, television programs without worrying about running out of tape space. The narrative was about what users could do with the format, not what the format could do.

A critical secondary factor was rental market adoption. JVC actively courted Hollywood studios to produce pre-recorded VHS movies, creating a library effect. Once Blockbuster and other rental stores committed to VHS, the installed base of renters created a self-reinforcing demand. Sony had not built these relationships.

### The Marketing Lesson

Sony's mistake was marketing the product. JVC's strategy was marketing the user outcome. "Betamax has better color" is a product claim. "You can record an entire basketball game or movie without changing tapes" is a user outcome claim.

For Chinese AI labs: the lesson is to stop making benchmark claims and start making outcome claims. "Our model scores 82% on SWE-bench Verified" is a product claim. "With our model, you can process an entire code repository in one pass and get a functional spec document out" is a user outcome claim. Product claims win benchmarks. Outcome claims win developer preference.

## Analogue 3: Craft Beer Movement — Identity as the Moat in a Fragmented Market

### The Arc

The US craft beer movement from 2010 to 2020 is the clearest analogue to the Chinese AI lab landscape. By 2020, there were approximately 8,000 craft breweries in the United States — none commanding more than 1-2% national market share. The three largest craft breweries (Doga, Duvel, and Boston Beer Company) collectively held less than 5% of the US beer market. There was no clear leader. The market was defined by fragmentation, rapid growth, and intense competition among thousands of brands.

During this period, craft beer grew from ~$8B to ~$22B in annual sales while the overall US beer market grew at ~2% annually. The growth was driven almost entirely by smaller, regional breweries that built dedicated local following — not by scale, distribution, or price competitiveness.

### What Actually Happened

**The competitive dynamic:** Macro breweries (Anheuser-Busch InBev, MillerCoors) had superior distribution networks, economies of scale, and retail relationships. Their products were cheaper per ounce at every volume tier. By any conventional competitive metric, craft breweries should have lost. They won anyway.

**How winning breweries differentiated:** Research across multiple craft beer markets in 2015–2020 shows three consistent marketing differentiation strategies among breweries that grew fastest:

1. **Local identity storytelling** — Breweries that built narratives around specific geographic origin, local ingredients, or regional history outperformed those that competed on beer style or price. Dogfish Head (Delaware) built narrative around "off-centered" ingredients and a quirky founder story. The Alchemist (Vermont) built around a single flagship beer (Heady Topper) and a regional cult following in New England. These stories were not interchangeable — each brewery owned a specific narrative identity that competitors could not replicate.

2. **Social media community-building over advertising** — Brewers who used Untappd, Instagram, and local event presence to build direct relationships with customers grew faster than those who invested in traditional advertising. The mechanism was peer recommendation: craft beer drinkers trusted recommendations from friends and online communities more than brand advertising. Brewers who hosted taproom events, shared brewing process content, and engaged with local beer communities created advocacy networks that no advertising budget could replicate.

3. **Narrative ownership over product features** — Rather than competing on ABV,ibu, or brewing technique, successful craft breweries claimed specific narrative territory. Some owned "sessionable low-alcohol beers for outdoor recreation." Others owned "imperial stouts aged in bourbon barrels." The key was that once a brewery owned a narrative space, competitors entering that space were perceived as derivatives. The first brewery to claim "the brewery for hikers" owned that identity in a way that a later entrant could not.

### The Marketing Lesson

In a market with thousands of competitors and no clear leader, product quality is table stakes — not a competitive advantage. The winning strategy is narrative identity: owning a specific, defensible position in the developer community's mind that competitors cannot easily replicate.

For Chinese AI labs: the question is not "how do we beat Qwen on benchmarks." The question is "which narrative space can we own first, and can we own it completely?" Options include:

- "The model that handles long legal documents without chunking"
- "The model that writes idiomatic code in your team's existing style"
- "The model that Korean enterprise developers trust for banking compliance"
- "The model that creative agencies use for multimedia content pipelines"

Each of these is a narrative position. Each is specific enough that a competitor claiming it would sound like a copycat. None require distribution advantages — only marketing focus and product consistency.

## Analogue 4: Korean Hallyu Wave — Network Identity Differentiation Without a Clear Leader

### The Arc

The Korean Wave (Hallyu) in television dramas is an instructive case because it involved three major broadcast networks — KBS, MBC, and SBS — competing simultaneously across multiple markets from 2003 to 2020, with no single network achieving dominant market share either domestically or internationally during the growth years.

The competitive dynamic was particularly intense because all three networks produced dramas on similar budgets, with similar production values, using similar star systems. In the early 2010s, each network was producing 20-30 dramas per year. The question for each network was how to differentiate when the core product was structurally similar.

### What Actually Happened

**How KBS differentiated:** KBS positioned itself as the network for historical dramas and heartland family narratives — stories about Korean history, traditional values, and intergenerational conflict. Its brand narrative centered on cultural authenticity and educational value. This differentiated it from the romantic comedies and melodramas favored by competitors. KBS dramas were the ones parents recommended to children.

**How MBC differentiated:** MBC owned the romantic melodrama and prestige drama space. Its brand narrative was emotional intensity and production scale. MBC dramas like "Moon Embracing the Sun" and "Descendants of the Sun" were the ones with the highest production budgets, the most internationally recognizable stars, and the most elaborate visual production. MBC owned the "premium romantic drama" narrative.

**How SBS differentiated:** SBS carved out the modern urban drama and trendy procedural space. Its dramas were set in contemporary Seoul, featured fashion-conscious young protagonists, and addressed contemporary social issues. SBS owned the "modern, trend-forward drama" narrative. When international streaming platforms (Netflix, DramaFever) entered the market, SBS content was most compatible with international viewing patterns.

**The marketing lesson from the Hallyu case:** All three networks competed in the same product category (Korean television drama), at similar price points (free-to-air, funded by advertising), with similar distribution (nationally and internationally via licensing). The differentiation was entirely narrative identity. Each network owned a specific emotional and thematic space that the others did not challenge directly.

### Why This Is Relevant

The Chinese AI lab landscape parallels the Korean broadcast landscape in this specific structure: multiple competitors making structurally similar products, no single player holding a dominant market share, each attempting to find a differentiating narrative. The winning strategy was not the best drama — it was the most clearly owned narrative identity.

For Chinese AI labs: the analogue suggests that each lab should pick one narrative identity and defend it exclusively, rather than trying to be the best at everything. DeepSeek should own "the open-source coding model." GLM should own "the research and academic model." MiniMax should own "the content creation and multimedia model." The moment each lab clearly owns a narrative space, competitor claims in that space sound like imitations.

## Synthesis: Marketing Lessons for Chinese AI Labs

### What the Four Analogues Agree On

| Analogue | Winner | Marketing Strategy | What It Was Not |
|----------|-------|--------------------|-----------------|
| Browser Wars II | Chrome | Performance as a verifiable claim (speed) | Open-source identity |
| VHS vs Betamax | VHS | User outcome narrative (freedom to record) | Technical superiority |
| Craft Beer | Regional identity brands | Local storytelling + community ownership | Price competition |
| Korean Hallyu Wave | Each network owned different identity | Narrative territory ownership | Same-product differentiation |

### The Core Principles

**1. Claim a specific narrative territory and defend it exclusively**

In every analogue, the winning player did not try to own the entire market. Chrome owned "fastest browser." VHS owned "the format for recording your life." KBS owned "historical Korean drama." MBC owned "premium romantic drama." SBS owned "modern urban drama." Each is a specific, defensible position.

Chinese AI labs must choose: which narrative space is ours? The decision should be driven by the lab's genuine strengths, not by what the competition is doing. If MiniMax's multi-modal capability is the strongest feature, own "the model for content workflows." If DeepSeek's MIT licensing and coding benchmarks are strongest, own "the open-source coding model." The specific territory matters less than the commitment to own it exclusively.

**2. Outcome claims beat product claims**

Betamax marketed the product. VHS marketed what you could do with it. The labs currently marketing SWE-bench scores are making product claims — they are describing the product, not the outcome. Developers care about outcomes: can they ship faster, can they process longer documents, can they reduce their API bill by 40%? The marketing that will resonate describes these outcomes, not the benchmark number.

**3. In fragmented markets, community is the moat**

Craft breweries that built taproom communities and used Untappd/Instagram to create peer recommendation networks grew faster than those that invested in advertising. Chrome leveraged Google's existing user base as a community platform. VHS leveraged the Hollywood rental ecosystem as a community signal.

For AI labs: the developer community itself is the marketing medium. A developer who posts "I switched to [Lab] and my inference costs dropped 40%" is more valuable than any advertisement. Labs should invest in making their community a place where developers want to share these results, not just use the API quietly.

**4. First-mover narrative ownership has compounding returns**

In the Korean Hallyu case, MBC's ownership of "premium romantic drama" was so strong by 2012 that when Netflix entered the international drama market in 2016, MBC content was its primary target for licensing. The narrative identity had created a content moat that was recognized globally.

For Chinese AI labs: the first lab to clearly claim and defend a narrative territory will build compounding advantages. The second lab to claim the same territory is a follower. The compounding mechanism is community: developers who have built integrations, prompts, and workflows on one lab's model are reluctant to switch, creating lock-in that makes the initial narrative position increasingly defensible.

### What This Report Does Not Address

This report is focused on marketing narrative differentiation. It does not address:
- Product strategy (which capabilities to build)
- Distribution strategy (which platforms to integrate with)
- Pricing strategy (how to compete on cost)
- Enterprise sales (how to negotiate contracts)

These are important dimensions that interact with marketing narrative — but the question asked was specifically about historical analogues for marketing strategy in a fragmented market with no clear leader.

## Limitations and Caveats

**1. Software products are more replicable than physical products**

Craft beer, VHS tapes, and broadcast dramas are physical or service products with genuine production costs and supply chain constraints. AI models can be replicated more quickly — training approaches are published in papers, and model weights may be available under MIT licenses. The narrative moats described in this report may be more durable in physical products than in AI services.

**2. Developer communities are global, not regional**

Craft breweries and Korean broadcasters competed in markets where geography created natural segmentation. Developer communities are global by default — a Chinese AI lab's developer community on GitHub, Reddit, or Discord spans US, EU, KR, and JP simultaneously. This creates both opportunities (global reach without physical distribution) and risks (global criticism spreads just as fast).

**3. The benchmark media environment may be structurally biased toward benchmarking**

Unlike the craft beer or Korean drama industries, the tech media and analyst ecosystem that covers AI models is specifically organized around benchmark comparison. This creates a media environment that rewards benchmarking and makes narrative differentiation harder to achieve through mainstream tech coverage. Labs may need to invest in alternative channels — community platforms, independent developer advocates, YouTube content — rather than relying on mainstream tech media.
