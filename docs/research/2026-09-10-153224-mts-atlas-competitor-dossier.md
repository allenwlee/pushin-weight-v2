# MTS and MTS Atlas competitor dossier

**Date:** 2026-09-10  
**Subject:** MTS Live / Monitoring the Situation and MTS Atlas  
**Primary properties:** [mts.now](https://www.mts.now/), [MTS Atlas](https://intelligence.mts.now/atlas), [@MTSlive](https://x.com/MTSlive)  
**Purpose:** establish MTS Atlas as PushinWeight's primary product benchmark and document the company, product, data system, distribution, business model, technical footprint, traction, limitations, and strategic implications.

This is competitor research, not a product plan or implementation SSOT. Product counts are a point-in-time observation and will move continuously.

---

## Executive assessment

MTS is a very young, a16z-backed San Francisco media-and-software company built around an unusually strong thesis: the most important technology story is the approach to AGI, the primary source is the live internet, and the winning media company will help people make sense of it continuously. It launched publicly on April 20, 2026 as an X-native live show. By September it had expanded into a multi-surface intelligence operation: live broadcasts, short clips, a daily newsletter, interactive research projects, searchable evidence collections, games, a public media API, a frontier-lab dashboard, and MTS Atlas.[^1][^2]

Atlas is the most important benchmark we have found for PushinWeight's emerging “stock-market dashboard for AI” direction. It is not merely a news feed. It joins five live systems:

1. **Situations:** X-native event clustering, synthesis, source roles, freshness, and momentum ranking.
2. **Models:** model news, benchmark standings, official and routed prices, release history, and adoption proxies.
3. **Companies:** entity profiles with descriptions, funding, people, news, and jobs.
4. **Investors:** portfolios, led rounds, capital totals, and recent investments.
5. **Jobs and profiles:** a 15,000-plus-role market with semantic search, saved jobs, local-first preferences, and optional account sync.[^3][^4][^5][^6][^7]

The company is a serious threat because it has combined three things that are usually separate: an audience, a newsroom, and a structured data product. The live show and clips generate reach and relationships; the newsroom turns source material into editorial products; the data system turns the same work into a reusable graph. MTS can distribute Atlas to an audience that already follows its coverage, while Atlas makes the media operation more useful and defensible.

The direct overlap with PushinWeight is substantial but incomplete. Both products monitor X, organize AI-lab discourse, synthesize posts, rank what matters, and expose source evidence. Atlas is broader and currently stronger as a horizontal AI-economy terminal. PushinWeight's defensible position remains deeper and more operational: a product made for one lab or a small set of labs, with complaints and requests separated from general chatter, per-brand context, 15-minute response cycles, and localized English/Simplified Chinese/Japanese understanding. Atlas tells an informed reader what is moving. PushinWeight should tell a specific team what needs a response and why.

**Bottom line:** benchmark Atlas's information architecture, evidence model, entity graph, freshness, and live-data presentation. Do not copy its horizontal scope. The opportunity is to become the vertical action layer that Atlas does not yet provide.

## 1. Company identity and thesis

### What MTS is

MTS stands for **Monitoring the Situation**. Its main site describes a live weekday technology news and talk show covering the people, products, markets, and online conversations shaping technology. The company and its X account increasingly use the sharper positioning **“Chronicling the singularity.”**[^2][^8]

The official a16z launch announcement called MTS a new media company and said a16z was seeding it alongside a group of angel investors. The original remit was broader than AI: technology, business, politics, and culture, discussed live on X with people close to the events. The underlying editorial idea was “sense-making”—following a fast-moving online event from multiple angles instead of waiting for a conventional newsroom package.[^1] Independent launch coverage likewise described it as a new a16z-associated online show.[^34]

By September, the practical center of gravity had narrowed toward AI and the physical/economic buildout around it. That focus is visible in the show, newsletter, research drops, Atlas categories, model data, hiring coverage, funding coverage, AI policy organizations, and the company's own hiring language.[^3][^9]

### Operating model

MTS resembles a compact newsroom/product studio rather than a conventional SaaS startup:

```text
live X show + interviews
          ↓
clips / posts / newsletter
          ↓
source relationships + audience + topic judgment
          ↓
structured situations, entities, jobs, funding, and model data
          ↓
Atlas, dashboards, research drops, evidence sites, games, API
```

This flywheel matters more than any single screen. High-status guests give the company primary material and distribution. Repeated daily production creates a disciplined monitoring operation. Atlas packages that operational knowledge into a product. Sponsors fund reach while product engineers build longer-term enterprise and consumer options.

## 4. Product portfolio beyond Atlas

Understanding the whole portfolio explains why Atlas is stronger than an isolated dashboard.

### Live show and replay archive

The main site is organized around the live state: current episode, live/replay player, featured guests, schedule, sponsors, and recent research drops. The show airs weekdays and is distributed on X and YouTube. On the audited day, episode 90's public schedule ran from 09:00 to 15:45 Pacific with multiple 15–30 minute guest blocks, a discourse review, and a “State of the Timeline” segment.[^2][^19]

The archive is deeply structured. Episodes connect to individual guests and timestamped chapters. Chapter records include titles, summaries, guest identities, and dedicated video clips. This converts hours-long live programming into searchable, reusable media objects rather than treating the broadcast as an endpoint.[^14]

### Newsletter

The Substack publishes near-daily briefings with a compact list of the day's important stories, expert/guest lists, and selected social posts. The archive began the same day as the show and had reached 12,000 subscribers and 122 posts by this audit.[^15]

### Drops and evidence products

`drops.mts.now` is an archive of 22 public interactive research projects spanning technology, finance, health, geopolitics, and law. Examples include AI-capex economics, the memory supply chain, model tokenization, world models, AI research papers, startup-language trends, congressional portfolios, and a Tokyo AI corridor. Several products expose primary documents, methodology, or live calculators rather than merely presenting an essay.[^20]

Other live properties include:

- **MTS Evidence:** searchable legal-document collections, including Anthropic v. Department of War, Facebook materials, and Musk v. Altman.
- **MTS Trial Wiki:** a dedicated Musk v. Altman case surface.
- **MTS UFO File:** a searchable/geocoded interface over declassified government documents.
- **Games:** a weekly tech quiz and a daily semantic “Latent Space” game.

This portfolio shows an editorial pattern worth noting: when an event has enough source material, MTS builds a small data product around it. The product is the reporting format.[^21]

### Public API and agent readiness

The main MTS media property publishes an OpenAPI 3.1 specification with eight unauthenticated read operations: live configuration, episode list/latest/detail, guest list/detail, schedule, and situations. It also publishes `llms.txt` describing two agent capabilities—checking live status and browsing the public archive. The API uses machine-readable errors and short cache lifetimes appropriate to each resource.[^22][^23]

Observed API scale:

- 90 episodes
- 810 chapters
- 999 guest-directory entries
- 50 current situations
- 10 active sponsor records

No MCP endpoint or manifest was found at the usual `mcp`, `mcp.json`, or `.well-known/mcp.json` locations on either the main or Atlas domain. Atlas itself has no public `llms.txt` or OpenAPI specification. Its browser uses multiple accessible JSON endpoints, but those are undocumented application APIs, not a declared third-party developer contract.

## 5. Atlas product teardown

### Positioning and information architecture

The Atlas PWA manifest calls the product **“Decision-grade intelligence on the AI economy.”** The global navigation is unusually spare: Situations, Jobs, Companies, Models, Investors, Profile, and Sign in. Atlas is publicly browsable without an account.[^24]

The design uses a terminal/research aesthetic: dense tables, terse labels, freshness stamps, compact source attribution, restrained color, and strong numeric hierarchy. It does not hide the database under a decorative marketing page. The data is the page.

### 5.1 Situations

Situations is the surface with the greatest direct overlap with PushinWeight. It presents 50 current stories and says they are ranked by source quality and momentum. Each situation can include:

- synthesized headline and summary;
- genre, criticality, and lifecycle;
- event type and one-sentence event phrase;
- entities and topics;
- “what changed” revision summary;
- source count and momentum multiple;
- source posts with account, timestamp, text, engagement, percentile, and evidence role.[^4][^25]

The public JSON is more revealing than the UI. Every story includes a rank breakdown with decay, velocity, diversity, total likes, source weight, source breadth, evidence quality, significance, and a single-source penalty. Evidence posts are assigned roles such as `origin`, `official_confirmation`, `report`, `reaction`, and `analysis`.

At the audited moment, the 50 stories contained 128 evidence posts, an average of 2.56 sources per story, with a maximum of 27. Fourteen stories had multiple sources; 36 had only one. Criticality was 1 Critical, 7 Elevated, and 42 On Watch. Forty-nine were labeled `confirmed`, even though 35 of those confirmed stories had only one source and an inferred-confirmation flag. This means “confirmed” should not be read as “independently corroborated.” It more likely means the system judged the post to be evidence that the described event or statement occurred.

That caveat aside, the evidence model is excellent. Atlas distinguishes the first claim, official confirmation, journalism, analysis, and reaction while keeping them inside a single evolving situation. This is a more useful information object than either a bare post or an untraceable AI summary.

### 5.2 Jobs

The default `/atlas` page is the job board. It contained roughly 15,400 current roles during the audit and updates frequently enough for the total to change within minutes. The board spans frontier labs, new labs, world models, agents, inference, neoclouds, chips, physical AI, AI for science, applications, research organizations, policy organizations, science policy, and funders.[^3]

Important interaction patterns:

- natural-language search prompts such as model-evaluation work in a city;
- location, discipline, category, and sort filters;
- salary normalization where available;
- job cards linked back to company profiles;
- save and “more jobs like this” actions;
- separate “Job board” and “Hiring on X” tabs;
- current-role matching beneath X hiring posts;
- local-first saved jobs and preferences, with optional account synchronization.

Observed job links show direct ingestion from Ashby, Greenhouse, Lever, and employer career pages. The client also references LinkedIn and X hiring feeds. The job product is strategically smart: it gives MTS a high-intent recurring use case, makes company pages useful between news events, and creates a plausible recruiting or sponsored-opportunity business.

### 5.3 Companies

Atlas indexed 424 companies and organizations. This is not just a venture-company database: it includes frontier labs, physical-AI companies, policy institutes, scientific organizations, safety groups, research institutes, government bodies, and funders.[^5]

Company pages can include:

- external website, X, careers, and newsroom links;
- founded date, headquarters, ownership, current job count, valuation, and funding count;
- timeline;
- “what it does” synthesis;
- observed hiring period and open-role context;
- tabs for jobs, funding, key people, and news;
- source links for funding and news.

The entity page is the connective tissue of Atlas. A source post becomes a situation; the situation names companies and people; company pages connect to funding, investors, models, and jobs. The result is an explorable graph even though the UI presents conventional pages.

### 5.4 Investors

The investor surface contained 2,878 profiles, exactly matching the sitemap breakdown: 2,638 venture firms, 58 corporate investors, 32 crossover/sovereign investors, and 150 angels. It ranks investors by tracked portfolio companies and shows round participation, rounds led, attributed led-round totals, and most recent lead.[^7][^26]

This is far broader than PushinWeight's current remit, but it matters strategically. MTS is modeling the AI economy as capital allocation, hiring, technical performance, product releases, and discourse—not merely a set of news articles.

### 5.5 Models

The Models surface has four tabs: News, Leaderboard, Pricing, and Adoption.[^6]

**News** associates stories with affected labs and preserves source links plus corroborating-source counts. The page showed 56 current stories during one observation; the older/parallel dashboard showed 60 moments later. Counts per lab are non-exclusive because one story can affect multiple labs.

**Leaderboard** presented Epoch's ECI with published confidence intervals and SimpleBench. The backing endpoint held 196 standings across eight benchmark sources: Aider, ARC-AGI, ARC-AGI-3, Arena, Epoch ECI, SimpleBench, SWE-bench Verified, and Terminal-Bench. It also generated seven-day rank movements.

**Pricing** distinguishes direct official prices from routed prices. The UI showed 130 current models observed within 30 days across seven official lab price lists, organized into frontier, balanced, and fast comparison tiers. The accessible pricing endpoint contained 973 records across current and historical/provider variants.

**Adoption** is unusually disciplined about denominators. It offers:

- Vercel AI Gateway token, request, and spend shares;
- OpenRouter model-token usage;
- Epoch public polling waves.

Each view states that it measures its source universe rather than the whole market. Epoch polling waves are kept separate when samples and populations differ. That methodological copy is a best-in-class pattern for any proxy metric.

### 5.6 Profile and personalization

The unauthenticated Profile page stores identity, ranked preferences, and saved jobs in the browser. It offers a curated job feed immediately, then invites the user to create an account to sync the state across devices. Sign-up is email/password. This reduces onboarding friction and gives visitors a working product before asking for registration.[^27]

The page says the board “starts learning” after the user saves jobs or ranks picks, but the public interface does not explain the recommendation method. Personalization currently appears job-centric; no public saved-situation, watchlist, or brand-alert workflow was found.

The prominent Neon and Databricks marks also appear in the active sponsor API. They are clearly partners/sponsors; the exact commercial and infrastructure terms are not public.[^24][^28]

### Observable collector architecture

Atlas exposes collector freshness records in its application JSON. Those records strongly imply separate scheduled collectors for:

- lab careers and job boards;
- official pricing from Anthropic, DeepSeek, Google/Gemini, Mistral, OpenAI, Qwen, and xAI;
- third-party pricing from Artificial Analysis;
- model evaluations from Aider, ARC-AGI, Arena, Artificial Analysis, Epoch, Hugging Face, SimpleBench, SWE-bench, and Terminal-Bench;
- adoption from Vercel, OpenRouter, Epoch polling, and a currently disabled Ramp integration;
- official model releases and lab news;
- X posts used for situations and hiring signals.

The system is therefore best understood as a set of source-specific collectors feeding a normalized Postgres/Databricks model, with Next.js composing public views and X-native editorial classification building higher-level situations.

Combined with OpenAPI and `llms.txt`, this indicates deliberate agent discoverability without granting training rights.[^23][^29]

## 7. Business model and distribution

### What is verified

- a16z and named angels funded the company at launch; amount and valuation were not disclosed;
- MTS carries sponsors and exposes 10 currently active sponsor records: Blitzy, AdQuick, Kong, ElevenLabs, Neon, Databricks, Arena, VCX, Lovable, and MongoDB;
- the main show, archive, newsletter preview, public API, research drops, and Atlas are currently publicly accessible;
- no public Atlas pricing page or paid tier was found;
- the company is hiring sales as well as product, editorial, production, and talent roles;
- the CEO says the product team is building proprietary enterprise and consumer products.[^1][^9][^28]

### Likely model, clearly marked as inference

The present business appears to be **sponsor-funded media plus venture-funded product development**. Atlas and jobs increase repeat utility and give sponsors an association with infrastructure and AI-market intelligence. Over time, the natural paid products are enterprise intelligence, recruiting, alerts, data access, or sponsored opportunity placement. This is an inference from product shape and hiring language; MTS has not publicly announced an Atlas subscription or enterprise contract.

The separation between `www.mts.now`, `drops.mts.now`, `dash.mts.now`, and `intelligence.mts.now` also suggests a portfolio strategy: the show is the audience hub, Drops is editorial research, Dash/Atlas is the recurring utility, and dedicated microsites are event-specific acquisition surfaces.

## 8. Data quality, transparency, and risks

Atlas is impressive, but “decision-grade” should be treated as a product aspiration rather than an audited guarantee.

### Strengths

- Source posts remain inspectable.
- Evidence roles separate origin, confirmation, reporting, reaction, and analysis.
- Situation rankings expose source breadth, engagement, velocity, decay, and quality factors.
- Model adoption views state their denominator and limitations.
- Pricing links to official sources and shows fetch dates.
- Funding/news items link to evidence.
- Freshness is visible across the product.

### Observed weaknesses

1. **“Confirmed” is broader than corroborated.** In one snapshot, 35 confirmed situations had exactly one source and inferred confirmation. A single post can therefore become a confirmed story.
2. **Entity association can be noisy.** Examples included a Cloudflare Workers item associated with Meta, a Tesla manufacturing item associated with xAI, and a robotics-kit discussion associated with OpenAI. Some may reflect an affected-lab relationship, but the UI does not explain why.
3. **Funding normalization has at least one serious error.** DeepSeek's page showed two June 2026 rounds of roughly $7.4 billion but displayed valuation figures of $50 billion and $350 billion. The linked reporting describes a valuation around CNY 330–350 billion, roughly USD 46–50 billion; the $350 billion display is consistent with a currency-labeling/normalization mistake.[^30][^31]
4. **Counts can be semantically confusing.** The dashboard reports hundreds of releases, tens of thousands of pricing changes, and hundreds of ranked models, while the focused UI shows smaller current subsets. These may all be correct, but definitions are not always adjacent to the headline number.
5. **Collector internals are publicly exposed.** Application responses reveal collector names, success/failure timestamps, records written, lease fields, and a disabled Ramp collector with a missing-key error. This is useful for research but leaks operational state.
6. **The legal/commercial surface is thin.** No readily discoverable Atlas terms, privacy page, methodology document, pricing page, or corporate legal name was found during this audit.
7. **The product is extremely new.** There is no public evidence yet for retention, paid conversion, enterprise adoption, alert quality, or performance under sustained user load.

### Competitive implication of the weaknesses

Atlas has prioritized breadth and shipping speed. That produces a large, compelling graph quickly, but it also creates normalization and classification risk. PushinWeight should not try to beat that breadth. It can win by making a smaller set of classifications operationally trustworthy for the specific team receiving them.

## 9. MTS Atlas vs PushinWeight

| Dimension | MTS Atlas | PushinWeight | Competitive reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core promise | Understand the AI economy and find AGI-relevant opportunities | Know which complaints and requests matter to a specific AI lab; organize the rest | Different job-to-be-done despite feed overlap |
| Scope | Horizontal: labs, infrastructure, science, policy, capital, jobs | Vertical: a small set of target AI labs and their X conversations | PushinWeight must preserve depth |
| Primary user | AI worker, founder, investor, researcher, informed reader | Lab developer-relations, product, support, and leadership staff | PushinWeight's buyer/user is narrower and more actionable |
| Unit of value | Situation, entity, model, company, investor, or job | Actionable post/cluster for one brand | Atlas informs; PushinWeight should route action |
| Sources | X plus official price/release/benchmark/adoption/jobs/funding sources | Primarily X, deeply classified and localized | Atlas wins breadth; PushinWeight can win interpretation |
| Ranking | Source quality, momentum, engagement, decay, significance | Brand relevance, post type, sentiment, discourse/aspects, nationalism, recency | Combining trust and urgency is the key lesson |
| Evidence | Explicit source roles and expandable post evidence | Original post, metadata, synthesis, classification signals | Atlas sets the evidence bar |
| Localization | English public UI; global organizations and some China data | English, Simplified Chinese, and Japanese direction; China-specific pragmatics | Strong PushinWeight differentiator |
| Personalization | Jobs/preferences saved locally or to account | Brand- and user-specific windows/filters | PushinWeight should be organization-first, not just user-first |
| Distribution | ~490k X audience, live show, clips, newsletter, guests, a16z network | Early direct outreach to a few target labs | MTS has the overwhelming reach advantage |
| Agent interface | Main media OpenAPI + `llms.txt`; no Atlas MCP/docs found | Agent-facing opportunity remains open | Atlas has declared intent but incomplete Atlas parity |
| Monetization | Sponsorship verified; future enterprise/consumer products signaled | Not assessed in this dossier | Atlas can subsidize product with media reach |

### Where Atlas is ahead

1. A coherent entity graph across discourse, companies, people, funding, investors, jobs, and models.
2. Evidence roles and source-level corroboration inside a story object.
3. Live freshness and momentum communicated directly in the interface.
4. Multiple model-adoption proxies with explicit denominator caveats.
5. Distribution: its product launches into an existing AI-native audience.
6. A newsroom capable of making bespoke interactive research products quickly.
7. Public machine-readable media infrastructure from day one.

### Where PushinWeight can be meaningfully better

1. **Action, not ambient awareness.** A complaint about a broken API, a time-sensitive event question, or a concrete feature request should be elevated above general AI news.
2. **Customer-of-one context.** MiniMax, Qwen, Z.ai, and DeepSeek do not need the same feed, terminology, thresholds, language, or escalation logic.
3. **Multilingual pragmatics.** Translation alone is insufficient. Chinese, Japanese, and English posts carry different politeness, nationalism, sarcasm, and implied urgency.
4. **Operational completeness.** The important outcome is not “a situation exists”; it is “the right person saw it, understood the implication, and responded or consciously monitored it.”
5. **Precision over coverage.** Atlas's observed errors are the predictable tax of a horizontal graph. A narrow system can earn trust by being right on the few classifications that trigger action.
6. **Per-lab briefing and auditability.** Every synthesis should answer why the post matters to this lab, which evidence supports that conclusion, and how confidence was assigned.

## 10. What to copy, what to reject, what to watch

### Copy as a benchmark

- Make the live data itself the marketing proof; avoid a generic dashboard screenshot.
- Treat a situation as an evolving object with evidence, revisions, and source roles.
- Put freshness and provenance beside the claim.
- State denominators and proxy limitations directly in the UI.
- Use entity pages to join posts, models, people, and events.
- Let a visitor experience saved/personalized value before forcing sign-up.
- Keep a machine-readable public surface aligned with the human one.
- Build distribution and product as one system, even if PushinWeight's distribution is direct sales rather than media.

### Do not copy

- The 400-company / 2,878-investor horizontal scope.
- A generic “everything happening in AI” product promise.
- Treating engagement as equivalent to customer importance.
- A confirmation label that users may mistake for independent corroboration.
- Dense terminal aesthetics without a clear response hierarchy.
- Model, hiring, or funding data that does not change a target customer's next action.

### Leading indicators that MTS is becoming a direct competitor

Monitor for:

1. saved situations, company watchlists, or brand-specific alerting;
2. enterprise or team workspaces;
3. paid Atlas tiers, recruiting products, or intelligence contracts;
4. webhook, supported Atlas API, MCP, or other agent delivery;
5. complaint/request/customer-support classification;
6. organization-specific routing or response workflows;
7. Chinese/Japanese interface or non-English source expansion;
8. product-engineering hires describing proprietary alerting or enterprise intelligence.

If two or more of the first six appear, Atlas will have crossed from the best adjacent benchmark into a direct product competitor.
